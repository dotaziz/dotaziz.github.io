Certain issues are extremely sensitive because they touch deeply held moral values, historical trauma, identity, and human dignity.

- Racial slurs and racism
- Blasphemy and hate speech
- Human-rights abuses and slavery
- Terrorism and extremist violence
- Nazi symbolism, Holocaust denial, and antisemitism
- Genocide and war crimes
- Sexual violence and child abuse

These issues tend to evoke strong emotions, which is one reason societies have developed powerful social taboos around them.

But these taboos aren't all the same. Some exist because certain things represent dehumanization or discrimination; others are connected to horrific historical events, religious beliefs, or direct harm to people.

That's why certain statements can trigger a reaction far beyond the literal words themselves. They can be perceived as crossing a deeply established moral boundary.

Don't be surprised if you use a racial slur in certain parts of the East Oakland and provoke an extremely hostile reaction. That doesn't justify violence, of course, but it demonstrates how deeply some words can become socially taboo because of the history and meaning attached to them.

The same principle applies to religious blasphemy.

Now consider the recent videos in Ghana, involving an Imam calling for the killing of people who insult or denigrate the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, apparently in response to a video in which someone insulted the Prophet ﷺ.

I am not justifying the call for violence, but hear me out.

As Muslims, we should not pretend that an insult against the Prophet ﷺ is insignificant. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ occupies an exceptionally sacred position in Islam. We love him deeply, and an insult against him is naturally going to cause profound anger and hurt among Muslims.

Someone who understands the position of the Prophet ﷺ in Islam should understand why Muslims are deeply disturbed by such speech.

But understanding the anger is not the same as justifying every reaction to it.

There is a fundamental distinction between:

“What this person said is deeply offensive and morally reprehensible.”

and:

“Therefore, I personally have the right to kill him.”

Those are two completely different propositions.

And this distinction goes beyond blasphemy.

Imagine someone genuinely believes that a government is corrupt, unjust, or has committed serious wrongdoing. That belief alone does not give every individual the authority to overthrow the government through violence or simply declare themselves entitled to use force.

There is a distinction between criticizing authority, seeking political change, and taking the law into one's own hands through violence.

Likewise, if someone believes that another person has committed a serious religious or legal offence, that belief does not automatically give them the authority to impose punishment themselves.

This is where legitimate authority and due process become important.

If every individual is allowed to determine guilt and administer punishment according to his own interpretation of religious law, then there is no longer a functioning legal order. There is simply vigilantism.

And this principle is not unique to Islamic law.

A functioning society cannot operate on:

“I believe you committed a crime, therefore I am entitled to punish you.”

Nor can it operate on:

“I believe the government is unjust, therefore I am entitled to overthrow it by force.”

There have to be legitimate mechanisms through which disputes, crimes, political grievances, and alleged abuses of power are addressed.

This is also an important principle within the classical Islamic legal tradition. Serious judicial punishments were matters for legitimate political and judicial authority, not something ordinary individuals were entitled to impose upon whomever they believed had committed an offence.

The same broader principle applies to maintaining political order. Islamic jurisprudence has extensive discussions concerning legitimate authority, rebellion, public disorder, justice, and the circumstances under which political authority may be challenged. These are matters of law and political jurisprudence, not decisions for individuals to make impulsively based on anger.

All of this ultimately comes back to maintaining order.

We should strongly condemn the insult against the Prophet ﷺ while also strongly condemning vigilantism and incitement to violence.

If a genuine offence has occurred, the appropriate authorities should handle it through the applicable legal process.

We should condemn blasphemy without endorsing murder.

We should understand the anger without endorsing violence.

We should defend the sanctity of the Prophet ﷺ without defending vigilantism.

And we can believe a government is unjust without believing that every individual therefore has a personal mandate to overthrow it through violence.

These positions are not contradictory.

In fact, separating these questions is precisely what allows us to uphold both religious conviction and social order.

Law exists partly because human beings are emotional, tribal, and capable of acting on anger. Without legitimate authority and due process, every grievance can become a justification for retaliation, and every accusation can become a pretext for violence.

The objective should be justice over vengeance, lawful accountability over vigilantism, and order over chaos.

May Allah SWT forgive us, guide us to what is right, and grant us wisdom, justice, and restraint.
