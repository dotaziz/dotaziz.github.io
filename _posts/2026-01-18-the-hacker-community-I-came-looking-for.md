I am the organizer for GDG on Campus, UCC, and I'd like to take the chapter to another level within the Computer Science and Information Technology department and the school at large. To a level where students take what the chapter does seriously, where faculty members genuinely support it, where the chapter becomes the go-to hacker community on campus, and where the university management recognizes the community.

### The Image That Got Shattered

The first time I stepped into the university, my mental image of an ideal hacker community on campus was shattered.

The image of weekly meetups where continuing students would talk about and share what they'd built or were working on. Shattered.

The image where I'd find another fresher working on something interesting. Shattered.

The image where I'd meet people to discuss deep technological ideas. Shattered.

The image where I'd find people who were _wayyyy_ more advanced and enthusiastic about tech. Shattered.

The university community looked as though no one really cared. What students cared about was going to class and passing exams. My mates didn't follow tech news and didn't know what tool or technology was making waves that week.

I still remember when, in a class (Software Suite), the lecturer asked which tech news was making waves. Almost no one had an idea — this was in 2023, and the AI hype had already caught fire. I told him it was about the [Google antitrust lawsuit](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Google_LLC_(2023)>) (it didn't seem to be on his radar). He was more interested in the news that [ChatGPT had passed law school exams](https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/chatgpt-passes-law-school-exams-despite-mediocre-performance-2023-01-25/) and what that meant for lawyers.

Maybe others knew but didn't want to speak up. My belief is that 99% of the class (roughly 265 students) knew nothing.

The tipping point was joining my first GDSC (Google Developer Student Club, now GDGoC) session and seeing fewer than 10 people show up. That was it. The image was completely shattered.

### Where Things Stand Now

This experience has played out consistently throughout my time on campus (up to present, L400). I'd say about 20% of my mates have changed, and we can now have decent conversations about tech. But amongst the frustrations, I've noticed a few people who are actually good, especially among the current L300s.

There also seems to be a pattern: every year there's a freshman who is actually talented, who probably expected to find a hacker community on campus, and ended up disappointed, just like I was on my first day.

### The Case for a Real Hacker Community

Not everyone grew up with a computer in their hands, and that's okay. Some students arrive on campus having never written a line of code, never tinkered with hardware, never stayed up late debugging something they built themselves. That's not a failure on their part. It's just the reality of where they came from.

But those people need to be around others who did. People who can pull them in, show them the ropes, and make the path feel less daunting. Because passion for technology is rarely something you develop in isolation. It usually comes from seeing someone else do something that blows your mind, from a conversation that sparks something, from an environment that makes you feel like this is your world too.

That's what a real hacker community does. It's not just for people who already know things. It's for everyone who's curious, who wants to learn, who wants to build. A place where you can be inspired, and where you can inspire others.

For the hackers, it means finally having people to talk to. People who get excited about the same things, who push back on your ideas, who build alongside you. A community where you can share what you're working on without having to explain why it matters.

For the wannabe hackers, it means having a door into a world that might otherwise feel closed. It means seeing someone two levels ahead of you and thinking, "I could get there." It means having mentors who aren't formal mentors, just people you hang around who happen to know a lot.

This is a win-win. Because to foster growth, you need an environment that enables growth. Just like a plant needs the right soil to grow.

This is my vision for GDG on Campus, UCC: a community that enables the growth of hackers and wannabe hackers alike.

So help me, Lord.
